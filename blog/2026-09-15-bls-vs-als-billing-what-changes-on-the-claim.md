BLS and ALS ambulance transports may look similar on the surface, but they can require different billing codes and supporting documentation. The difference is not simply the title or certification level of the crew. The claim should reflect the level of service provided and what is supported by the patient care report.

Understanding these differences can help EMS agencies reduce billing errors and avoid submitting claims that do not match the documented service.

![BLS vs. ALS Ambulance Claims](/img/uploads/2026/09/bls-vs-als-ambulance-claims.png)

## What Is BLS Billing?

Basic Life Support (BLS) ambulance services generally involve transportation where the patient's condition and care requirements are supported by BLS-level services.

For Medicare ambulance claims, BLS codes include A0428 for non-emergency BLS transport and A0429 for emergency BLS transport.

The documentation should support the level of service reported on the claim. Patient condition, assessment, transport circumstances, and services provided should be clearly recorded in the patient care report.

## What Is ALS Billing?

Advanced Life Support (ALS) billing applies when the transport meets the applicable requirements for ALS-level service.

Medicare's ambulance fee schedule identifies A0426 for ALS Level 1 non-emergency transport and A0427 for ALS Level 1 emergency transport. ALS Level 2 is reported with A0433.

The important point is that an ALS claim needs appropriate documentation to support the level of service being billed. The presence of an ALS-capable vehicle or crew does not, by itself, mean that every transport should automatically be billed as ALS.

## What Changes on the Claim?

### 1. The HCPCS Code

The most obvious difference is the ambulance service code.

A BLS transport may use A0428 or A0429, depending on whether the applicable transport is non-emergency or emergency. ALS Level 1 may use A0426 or A0427, while ALS Level 2 uses A0433 under Medicare billing rules.

Choosing the code should be based on the actual service provided and the applicable payer requirements.

### 2. Supporting Documentation

The patient care report needs to support the level of service billed.

For ALS claims, documentation should clearly describe the patient's condition, assessment, treatments, procedures, medications, monitoring, and other relevant care provided during the transport.

If the claim indicates a higher level of service but the documentation does not support it, the payer may question or deny the claim.

### 3. Emergency vs. Non-Emergency Status

BLS and ALS each have emergency and non-emergency codes under Medicare.

For example, A0429 represents BLS emergency transport, while A0428 represents BLS non-emergency transport. For ALS Level 1, A0427 is used for emergency transport and A0426 for non-emergency transport.

This distinction means the billing team needs to review the transport circumstances rather than selecting a code based only on the crew or vehicle.

### 4. Mileage Still Needs Attention

The service level is not the only part of an ambulance claim that needs review.

Ground ambulance mileage is separately represented by the applicable mileage code under Medicare billing requirements. CMS identifies A0425 as ground mileage per statute mile.

The mileage reported should agree with the applicable documentation and payer requirements. A correct BLS or ALS service code does not make an inaccurate mileage entry acceptable.

### 5. Modifiers and Claim Details

Ambulance claims also contain origin and destination information through the applicable modifiers.

These details need to match the actual transport. An incorrect modifier can create a mismatch between the patient care report and the submitted claim.

## Common BLS and ALS Billing Problems

Problems often occur when the billing team relies on incomplete information or assumes that the crew level automatically determines the billing level.

Other issues can include:

- BLS and ALS codes being confused
- Emergency and non-emergency codes being mixed up
- Documentation not supporting the billed service
- Mileage being reported incorrectly
- Origin and destination modifiers not matching the transport
- Required claim information being missing

These issues are easier to address when the ePCR is reviewed before the claim is submitted.

## How EMS Agencies Can Improve Claim Accuracy

A consistent review process can help connect the clinical record with the claim. Before submission, the billing team can compare the documented patient condition, services performed, transport type, mileage, and origin and destination information with the claim details.

The objective should not be to automatically bill a higher level. It should be to make sure the claim accurately represents the service that was provided and is supported by the documentation.

For EMS agencies that need additional billing support, professional ambulance billing services can help with claim preparation, coding review, documentation checks, and follow-up on billing issues.

## Final Thoughts

BLS and ALS billing differ mainly in the level of service reported and the documentation required to support that service. Selecting the correct HCPCS code, confirming emergency status, checking mileage, and matching claim details with the patient care report are all important parts of the process.

When the documentation and claim tell the same story, the billing team has a stronger foundation for accurate claim submission.
