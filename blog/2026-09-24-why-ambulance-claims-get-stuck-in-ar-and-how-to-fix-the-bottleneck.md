Unpaid ambulance claims can put pressure on an EMS agency's cash flow. Some claims remain in accounts receivable (A/R) because of missing documentation, coding errors, eligibility problems, or delayed payer follow-up. When these issues are not identified early, claims can remain unpaid longer than expected.

The best way to reduce A/R delays is to identify where claims are getting stuck and fix the underlying problem.

![Why Ambulance Claims Get Stuck in A/R — and How to Fix the Bottleneck](/img/uploads/2026/09/why-ambulance-claims-get-stuck-in-ar.png)

## 1. Identify Why Claims Are Still Unpaid

Start by reviewing outstanding claims and grouping them by their current status. Separate claims that are still processing from those that need additional information, have been denied, or require follow-up.

This helps the billing team focus on the actual reason for each delay rather than treating every unpaid claim the same way.

## 2. Check Documentation Before Submission

Ambulance claims depend on clear documentation of the transport and the medical necessity for the service. Missing or unclear patient care report (PCR) details can lead to additional requests, denials, or payment delays.

Review whether the record supports the level of service billed and includes relevant information about the patient's condition, transport, destination, and loaded mileage.

A consistent documentation review before submission can help prevent avoidable problems.

## 3. Review Coding and Claim Details

Incorrect codes, missing modifiers, inaccurate mileage, and inconsistent claim information can create billing issues.

Compare the submitted claim with the patient care report and other supporting records. Make sure the billed service matches the documented transport and applicable payer requirements.

If similar errors appear repeatedly, review the process that produces them instead of correcting each claim individually.

## 4. Follow Up on Denials Promptly

A denied claim should have a clear next step. Review the denial reason, determine whether corrected information or an appeal is appropriate, and assign responsibility for follow-up.

Keep a record of the action taken and any response from the payer. This prevents claims from being overlooked while staff handle newer submissions.

## 5. Track A/R by Age and Reason

Review outstanding claims by age, payer, and reason for delay. For example, claims awaiting documentation may need a different follow-up process from claims that have been denied or remain under payer review.

Use this information to identify recurring bottlenecks. If one payer or claim type regularly experiences delays, investigate whether the cause relates to submission quality, documentation, or follow-up procedures.

## 6. Strengthen Compliance Checks

Compliance and A/R management are closely connected. Missing records, unsupported coding, or inconsistent billing practices can result in denials, repayment requests, or additional reviews.

Qiaben's article [Ambulance Billing Compliance: Avoiding Audits and Penalties in EMS](/blog/ambulance-billing-compliance-avoiding-audits-and-penalties-in-ems) explains the importance of accurate documentation, internal claim audits, and consistent compliance procedures. These practices can also help EMS agencies identify billing issues before they lead to prolonged payment delays.

## 7. Create a Simple Follow-Up Routine

Set a regular schedule for reviewing unpaid claims. Each outstanding claim should have a current status, a documented reason for delay, an assigned person, and a next action.

Effective [ambulance billing services](/ambulance-billing) workflows rely on consistent claim monitoring rather than waiting until unpaid balances become difficult to manage.

## Final Thoughts

Ambulance claims often get stuck in A/R because of a combination of documentation gaps, coding errors, payer delays, and inconsistent follow-up. Reviewing these issues systematically helps EMS agencies understand where the bottlenecks occur.

By improving claim accuracy, addressing denials promptly, and monitoring outstanding balances regularly, agencies can build a more reliable reimbursement process.
