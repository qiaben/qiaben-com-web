Revenue Cycle Management (RCM) is not only about sending claims and collecting payments. A healthy revenue cycle depends on many small processes working together, from entering charges to verifying insurance, getting approvals, posting payments, and following up on unpaid balances.

Many practices track basic financial numbers but miss other important signs that can reveal where money and time are being lost.

![Top RCM KPIs Every Practice Should Monitor](/img/uploads/2026/09/top-rcm-kpis-every-practice-should-monitor.png)

## 1. Charge Entry Lag

Charge entry lag measures the time between when a service is provided and when the charge is entered into the billing system.

A long delay can push the entire billing process back. If charges are entered several days after a patient visit, the claim may also be submitted later than necessary.

For example, if a service is provided on Monday but the charge is not entered until Thursday, the practice has already lost several days in the revenue cycle.

### Why it matters

A practice should monitor:

- Average time between service and charge entry
- Charges entered within 24 hours
- Charges that remain unposted
- Departments or providers with frequent delays

Reducing charge entry delays can help the practice move claims through the system faster.

## 2. Payment Posting Turnaround Time

Getting a payment from an insurance company is not the final step. The payment still needs to be posted correctly to the patient's account.

Payment posting turnaround time measures how quickly payments are entered into the billing system after they are received.

Slow payment posting can make account balances appear inaccurate and make it harder for staff to understand the practice's current financial position.

### What to watch

A practice can track:

- Average time to post insurance payments
- Average time to post patient payments
- Number of unposted payments
- Electronic payments waiting for reconciliation
- Manual posting errors

Faster and more accurate payment posting gives the practice a clearer view of its actual cash position.

## 3. Denial Recovery Rate

A practice may track how many claims are denied, but another useful number is how many denied claims are successfully recovered.

The denial recovery rate shows how effectively the billing team turns denied claims into payments.

For example, if a practice receives 100 denied claims and successfully resolves 80 of them, its recovery performance is much different from a practice that resolves only 40.

### Why this KPI matters

A high recovery rate can indicate that the team has an effective process for:

- Reviewing denial reasons
- Correcting errors
- Resubmitting claims
- Appealing valid denials
- Following up with payers

It is also useful to track how much money is recovered from these claims, not just the number of claims.

## 4. Underpayment Rate

Not every payment problem comes from a denial.

Sometimes an insurance payer pays less than the amount the practice expected. These underpayments can be easy to miss, especially when there are many claims.

The underpayment rate helps identify how often the practice receives less than the expected reimbursement.

A practice can compare the expected payment with the actual payment and investigate significant differences.

### Common reasons for underpayments

- Incorrect payer contracts
- Incorrect fee schedules
- Payment processing errors
- Incorrect adjustments
- Contract terms not being applied correctly
- Missing or incorrect claim information

Monitoring this KPI can help practices identify revenue that may otherwise be overlooked.

## 5. Authorization Approval Rate

Some procedures require prior authorization before the service is provided.

Authorization approval rate measures how many submitted authorization requests are approved.

A low approval rate may point to problems with documentation, eligibility, payer requirements, or the authorization process itself.

### What practices can monitor

Track:

- Number of authorization requests
- Number approved
- Number denied
- Average authorization turnaround time
- Procedures with frequent authorization problems

This can help staff identify issues before they turn into payment problems.

## 6. Eligibility Verification Accuracy

Checking whether a patient has active insurance is important, but simply completing a verification is not enough.

The information also needs to be accurate.

Eligibility verification accuracy measures how often the information collected during verification matches the payer's actual coverage information.

Important details may include:

- Active coverage
- Deductible
- Copayment
- Coinsurance
- Benefit limitations
- Coverage dates
- Patient responsibility

For dental and medical practices, accurate eligibility information can help reduce unexpected patient balances and billing problems.

## 7. Aging Distribution of Accounts Receivable

Instead of looking only at total accounts receivable, practices should examine how balances are distributed by age.

For example, a dashboard can separate outstanding balances into:

- 0-30 days
- 31-60 days
- 61-90 days
- 91-120 days
- More than 120 days

This gives a clearer picture of where older balances are building up.

A practice may have a large total AR balance but still have healthy collections if most of that balance is recent. On the other hand, a smaller AR balance can be a concern if a large percentage is more than 90 or 120 days old.

## 8. Cost to Collect

Revenue is important, but practices should also understand how much they spend to collect that revenue.

Cost to collect measures the resources required to bring money into the practice.

These costs can include:

- Billing staff salaries
- Billing software
- Administrative expenses
- Outsourced billing fees
- Collection expenses
- Time spent on claim follow-up

This KPI can help practice managers understand whether their current billing process is efficient.

A process that collects more money but requires significantly more resources may not be as efficient as it first appears.

## Use RCM KPIs to Find Hidden Revenue Problems

The most useful RCM dashboard is not necessarily the one with the largest number of metrics. It is the one that helps a practice quickly understand what is happening and where action is needed.

By monitoring areas such as charge delays, payment posting, denial recovery, underpayments, authorization performance, payer behavior, aging, and appeal results, practices can discover problems that may not be visible through basic financial numbers alone.
