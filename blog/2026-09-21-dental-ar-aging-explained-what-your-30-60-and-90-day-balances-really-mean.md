Dental practices do not always receive insurance payments immediately after submitting claims. Some claims take longer to process, while others remain unpaid because of missing information, payer delays, or follow-up issues. These outstanding amounts are known as accounts receivable (A/R).

An A/R aging report helps dental practices understand how long payments have remained outstanding. It groups unpaid balances into time periods, commonly 30, 60, and 90 days. Reviewing these categories helps the practice identify delayed payments, prioritize follow-ups, and maintain healthier cash flow.

But what do these aging categories actually mean, and when should a dental practice take action?

![Dental A/R Aging Explained](/img/uploads/2026/09/dental-ar-aging-explained.png)

## What Is Dental A/R Aging?

Dental accounts receivable refers to money that a practice is waiting to collect from insurance companies or patients for services already provided.

An A/R aging report organizes these unpaid balances according to how long they have been outstanding. The report usually includes categories such as:

- 0-30 days
- 31-60 days
- 61-90 days
- Over 90 days

For example, if a dental practice has submitted an insurance claim and has not received payment after 45 days, that balance may appear in the 31-60-day category.

The purpose of this report is not simply to show how much money is unpaid. It helps the billing team understand which balances need attention and where collection delays may be occurring.

## What Do 30-Day A/R Balances Mean?

The 0-30-day category generally contains recently submitted claims and outstanding patient balances. Some of these amounts may still be within the payer's normal processing period.

However, this does not mean every balance should be ignored until 30 days have passed. Billing teams should confirm that claims have been received and accepted for processing.

During this period, staff should check claim submission status, review clearinghouse responses, and confirm that payments or acknowledgments are being recorded correctly.

If a claim is rejected because of incorrect information or a missing detail, the team should correct it promptly rather than allowing it to move into an older aging category.

**What to watch:** Recently submitted claims that have not been acknowledged, rejected claims awaiting correction, and patient balances that require communication.

## What Do 60-Day A/R Balances Mean?

When a balance reaches 31-60 days, it deserves closer attention. A claim that remains unpaid for this long may indicate a processing delay, an unresolved rejection, missing documentation, or a problem with the original submission.

At this stage, billing staff should check the payer's claim status and determine why payment has not been received. If additional records or information are required, the practice should respond as soon as possible.

Patient balances also need attention. For example, insurance may have processed a claim and assigned part of the remaining amount to the patient. If the explanation of benefits has been received, the practice can update the account and send an accurate statement.

**What to watch:** Claims with no payment update, requests for additional information, unresolved payer questions, and patient balances awaiting collection.

## What Do 90-Day A/R Balances Mean?

Balances in the 61-90-day and over-90-day categories are more concerning because they have remained unpaid for a longer period.

These balances may involve denied claims, incorrect insurance information, unresolved appeals, missed follow-ups, or patient accounts that have not responded to statements.

The billing team should investigate each account rather than treating all older balances the same way. A claim awaiting an appeal requires a different action from a patient balance that has already been transferred after insurance processing.

It is also important to review timely filing and appeal deadlines. Waiting too long can limit the practice's ability to correct or appeal certain claims.

**What to watch:** Repeated denials, unresolved appeals, missing documentation, missed deadlines, and balances that may be difficult to collect.

## Why Does Dental A/R Aging Matter?

A/R aging gives practice owners a clearer picture of their financial position. A practice may have completed many treatments and submitted numerous claims, but that does not mean the expected money has been collected.

When a large portion of receivables remains in older categories, the practice may experience cash flow pressure. It may also indicate that billing processes need improvement.

For example, if many claims remain unpaid beyond 60 days, the practice may need to review its submission process, payer follow-up schedule, or insurance verification procedures. Aging reports also help teams decide which accounts should receive attention first. Instead of following up randomly, staff can focus on older, higher-value, or time-sensitive balances.

## How Can Dental Practices Reduce A/R Aging?

Weekly reviews help billing teams identify balances that are moving into older categories before they become harder to collect.

Next, every outstanding account should have a clear status. Staff should know whether a claim is awaiting payer processing, rejected, denied, under appeal, or assigned to the patient.

Consistent follow-up is equally important. Claims should not remain untouched simply because the team has not received a response. Staff should follow the payer's available status-check procedures and maintain records of calls, messages, and actions taken.

Practices should also strengthen the processes that happen before claim submission. Accurate patient information, insurance verification, correct procedure coding, and complete documentation can reduce avoidable delays.

A consistent approach to billing, payment posting, and follow-up is essential. ## Track the Right A/R Metrics

Looking at total outstanding balances alone does not provide the complete picture. Dental practices should also monitor how receivables are distributed across aging categories.

Two useful measures are the percentage of A/R over 90 days and the average time taken to collect outstanding payments. Practices can also track collection trends, unresolved denials, and the number of claims awaiting follow-up.

Comparing these figures over time helps identify whether collection processes are improving. For example, a reduction in older balances may indicate that follow-up activities are becoming more effective.

The important thing is to review these numbers consistently and use them to guide practical improvements.

## Conclusion

Dental A/R aging helps practices understand where their unpaid revenue stands and how long it has been waiting for collection. The 30-day category usually contains newer balances, while 60-day balances need closer follow-up. Balances approaching or exceeding 90 days require more focused investigation and timely action.

By reviewing aging reports regularly, assigning clear follow-up responsibilities, and correcting billing issues early, dental practices can improve collections and maintain more predictable cash flow.
